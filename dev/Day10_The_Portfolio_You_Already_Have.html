A structured re-read of your own portfolio. Four steps. About 90 minutes. You come out the other end with a marked-up copy and the beginnings of your anchor stories.

There are no Dumb Questions

Q:What if I can't remember the project I wrote about?
A:That's exactly what the Mining Pass fixes — you re-read your own words with the dates and numbers in front of you, and the memory comes back. If a section still feels distant, talk to the colleague you worked with on it.
Q:I included something in the portfolio that I'm not actually proud of. Hide it?
A:No — reflect on it. The Distinction descriptors reward apprentices who can evaluate their own work critically. "In hindsight I'd have done this differently because…" is exactly the move that scores. Day 16 will rehearse it.
Q:What if one KSB in my portfolio is barely covered?
A:The assessor has already read it and decided to assess on what's there. They may probe a thin section in the discussion — that's an opportunity to fill it verbally, not a trap.
Q:The assessor asks something and my mind goes blank. What now?
A:Standard recovery: "Could I take a moment to pull up that chapter in my portfolio?" Take 15 seconds, find the page, answer from there. Asking for a moment is professional, not failure.
Q:I've changed my mind about how I wrote something. Can I correct it?
A:Yes — and it often scores. "On reflection, what I wrote in K12 understated the challenge; what actually happened was…" shows reflective thinking, which is what the discussion is for.
Q:What if they ask about something I never wrote about?
A:Fully normal. The assessor isn't limited to the portfolio. Describe the real workplace example verbally and tie it to the KSB out loud. The one answer to avoid: "I don't do that" or "I have no evidence". That's the major-fault answer — immediate Refer on the criterion. Always reframe instead: "I haven't led on that, but I've supported it by…" or "I haven't covered that directly, but the principle I'd apply is…"

10 minutes — the first portfolio-talk practice

Say it for real

Practise these out loud. The first time will feel slow; by the third pass, it flows.

Q1 — Tell me about your portfolio in 90 seconds.

The shape: role & workplace → KSB structure → two-or-three strongest illustrations by name → what they collectively show.

Try: "I'm a data technician in [team] at [organisation]. My portfolio is structured by KSB — each chapter covers one. The illustrations I'm most likely to talk about today are [story 1], [story 2] and [story 3]. Between them they cover most of the four content areas."

Q2 — Which piece of work are you most proud of, and why?

The shape: pick one anchor story → name what made it hard → name the result → land on what it taught you about the work, not about yourself.

Q3 — What's one thing in your portfolio you'd change now?

The shape: pick something specific → show thinking that's developed since you wrote it. This scores particularly well at distinction level — it evaluates your own work, which is what D2 asks for.

SHOW THIS OFF

The word portfolio should appear in the first 30 seconds of your opening answer — not vaguely, but as a concrete anchor: "in my portfolio, in the K6 chapter, I described…" Signals to the assessor: grounded, defensible, same page.

Bullet Points — the chapter in 30 seconds

  • The writing is done. Phase 2 is rehearsal, not creation.
  • The Portfolio Mining Pass. Skim, Mark, Capture, Anchor — in that order.
  • Strong illustrations have three traits. A result, specifics, a beat-by-beat story. Two-of-three minimum.
  • Four anchor stories, one per content area. Most discussion questions hit at least one.
  • The assessor has the portfolio, the mapping, and the question shapes. The discussion isn't about whether you did the work.
  • Defensible answers anchor. "In my portfolio in chapter X I described…" is the opening move.
  • No bluffing. Reach for the portfolio mid-answer; never invent.
