click by click — slice the data without formulas

Build a PivotTable

A PivotTable is the workhorse for analysis presentation. One click, four drop zones, and you can answer questions like "sessions per region per plan type" without writing a single formula.

Step 1 — Insert the PivotTable

  1. Click anywhere inside the Activity Table you loaded on Day 4.
  2. Insert → PivotTable. A dialog appears. Leave Source (Excel pre-filled tblActivity); set Destination to New Worksheet. Click OK.
  3. A new sheet (rename it Pivots) opens with an empty pivot on the left and the PivotTable Fields pane on the right.

Step 2 — Drag fields into the four drop zones

The Fields pane has four drop zones at the bottom: Rows (left-hand labels), Columns (top headings), Values (the cell calculations), and Filters (a top-of-table dropdown). To answer "sessions per region per plan type":

  • Drag region → Rows.
  • Drag plan_type → Columns.
  • Drag record_id → Values (Excel will say "Count of record_id").

You now have a small table: regions down the side, plans across the top, session counts in the cells.

turn counts into totals, days into months

Change values, group dates, sort

Change the aggregation

By default, PivotTables count text fields and sum numeric ones. To change it:

  1. In the Fields pane, click the dropdown arrow next to the field in the Values box (e.g. "Count of record_id").
  2. Click Value Field Settings…
  3. Choose the function you want: Sum, Average, Max, Min, Count, Count Numbers, etc.
  4. Click OK.

Example: swap record_id in Values for duration, set the function to Sum, then divide manually by 60 in a separate cell to convert to hours. Or use Average to get mean session length per region per plan.

Group dates by month or year

When you drop a date field into Rows or Columns, Excel often auto-groups it. If it doesn't, or if you want different grouping:

  1. Right-click any date value in the PivotTable.
  2. Click Group…
  3. In the dialog, tick the level(s) you want: Days, Months, Quarters, Years. (Ticking multiple builds a hierarchy.)
  4. Click OK. The dates now group into the chosen periods.

Sort largest first

Right-click any value cell → Sort → Sort Largest to Smallest. The pivot reorders by the value column you clicked. To filter to the top N: right-click a row label → Filter → Top 10… → set "Top" and the number you want.

SHOW THIS OFF

Whenever you change a Pivot, narrate what just happened. "I changed the aggregation from Count to Sum, then divided by 60 — so this column is now total hours, not total minutes." Pivots are powerful but invisible to the audience; saying what you did makes the audit trail audible.

Verify the pivot — Two-Route Check on totals

PivotTables fail silently. A miscounted filter, a hidden row, a Sum on a text-typed column — you can get a clean-looking pivot that's quietly wrong. Apply The Two-Route Check (the named device from Day 4) before you trust the headline number.

The check is simple: take any grand-total value in the pivot and reproduce it with a single SUMIFS or COUNTIFS against the raw table. If the pivot's grand-total cell reads 87 cancelled subscribers across all regions, then =COUNTIFS(tblSubs[status], "cancelled") against the source table should also return 87. If the two numbers disagree by even one row, the pivot has a hidden filter, a wrong field type, or a stray group level — and you go fix it before it leaves your screen.

Do it once per pivot. Note the result in your methodology log. The assessor will ask "how do you know that number is right?" and you'll have an answer.

make the pivot interactive — for Priya, not just for you

Slicers and timelines

A slicer is a clickable filter that sits next to your PivotTable. Priya clicks a button (e.g. "Premium") and the entire pivot — and any chart linked to it — updates instantly. No formulas, no faff.

Add a slicer

  1. Click anywhere inside your PivotTable.
  2. PivotTable Analyze → Insert Slicer (on the ribbon). A dialog lists every column in the source table.
  3. Tick the columns you want as slicers — typically region and plan_type. Click OK.
  4. Excel drops one slicer box per ticked column onto the sheet. Drag them where you want them.
  5. Click any button on a slicer to filter. Hold Ctrl to multi-select. Click the filter-clear button (top-right of each slicer) to reset.

Add a timeline slicer for date columns

  1. Click inside the PivotTable.
  2. PivotTable Analyze → Insert Timeline. The dialog lists date columns from the source.
  3. Tick watch_date → OK.
  4. A horizontal timeline appears. Drag the handles to select a date range; click the unit dropdown (top-right) to switch between Days / Months / Quarters / Years.

Connect one slicer to multiple PivotTables

Right-click the slicer → Report Connections… → tick every PivotTable you want it to filter. Now one click on the slicer filters the whole dashboard.

ASSESSOR FAVOURITE

Slicers connected to multiple PivotTables is a distinction-band feature — it shows you understand the dashboard as a system, not just isolated tables. When you demonstrate, say: "I'm connecting this slicer to both pivots so one click filters them together. That's what makes the dashboard interactive."

a chart that updates with the pivot

PivotCharts

A PivotChart is the visual twin of a PivotTable. Click a slicer and both update together. That's what makes a dashboard feel alive.

Insert a PivotChart

  1. Click anywhere inside your PivotTable.
  2. PivotTable Analyze → PivotChart. The chart-type dialog appears (same as the regular chart dialog).
  3. Pick the chart type — for "sessions by region", choose Clustered Column. Click OK.
  4. Excel drops the chart onto the sheet. It's linked to the pivot — changes to either propagate to both.

Tidying the chart

  • Chart title. Click on the auto-title, type the title you actually want. "Sessions by Region — Q4 2024".
  • Axis labels. Click the chart → Chart Design → Add Chart Element → Axis Titles → tick Primary Horizontal and Primary Vertical. Then click each to rename.
  • Field buttons. The grey buttons Excel adds to a PivotChart (e.g. "region", "plan_type") are useful for you but clutter for Priya. To hide: right-click any field button → Hide All Field Buttons on Chart.

When to use a PivotChart vs a regular chart

PivotChart when the chart needs to follow the slicers. Regular chart when you want a fixed visual that doesn't change (e.g. a year-on-year headline chart that's always the same view). For most dashboards, mix both: PivotCharts for interactive sections, regular charts for the static headlines at the top.

the reason we did the first three days in Power Query

Press Refresh

Yesterday's numbers, today's pivots, tomorrow's dashboard — all of it sits on top of the Power Query pipeline you built in the first three days. Which means when next month's data lands, you don't redo any of it. You press one button and the whole thing updates.

What Refresh actually does

Data → Refresh All (or Ctrl+Alt+F5). One click, in this order: Power Query re-reads every source file, the Cleaning Sequence reruns, the canonical key transformation reruns, merges and appends rerun, Tables on sheets update, every formula recalculates, every PivotTable rebuilds, every PivotChart redraws.

Total work for you: zero. Total time: usually under thirty seconds.

Why this is the killer feature

Without Power Query, when next month's data arrives you'd be opening files in Excel, copy-pasting, redoing your VLOOKUPs, fixing the dates, re-cleaning the genres, rebuilding the pivot, re-pointing the charts — a full afternoon at least, probably with a bug or two introduced. With Power Query, it's one click.

This is the actual business value of doing the upstream work properly. It's also the answer to one of the most common assessor questions: "why did you use Power Query rather than just doing this in Excel?"

a 3-question rule for picking a chart

The Chart Test

The single biggest mistake in dashboards is the wrong chart for the question. Three questions to ask before you pick:

  1. Comparison, composition, or trend? Comparing values across categories, showing parts of a whole, or tracking change over time?
  2. How many things? 2–10 categories work for almost any chart. 50+ usually need a table, not a chart.
  3. Is time the X-axis? If yes, line chart almost always. If no, bar or column.

A note on pie charts. Pie charts only work for ≤5 slices, with clearly different sizes, when the parts add up to a meaningful whole. Beyond five slices, the human eye can't compare them properly. Reach for a stacked bar instead.

ASSESSOR FAVOURITE

Asked "why this chart, not another?", walk through the chart test out loud. "It's a comparison across five categories with no time element, so a column chart is the right choice. A pie would technically work but bars are easier to compare at a glance."

Halfway House

Four scenarios. Pick the right chart for each. Answers below the line.

2. She wants to see how monthly sessions changed across the 12 months of FY 2025-26.

☐ Pie   ☐ Column / Bar   ☐ Line   ☐ Stacked bar

3. She wants to see what proportion of subscribers are Basic / Standard / Premium.

☐ Pie   ☐ Column / Bar   ☐ Line   ☐ Stacked bar

4. She wants to compare, for each of 11 regions, the split of sessions across Basic / Standard / Premium plans.

☐ Pie   ☐ Column / Bar   ☐ Line   ☐ Stacked bar

Answers — check yourself

1. Column / Bar. Comparison across 11 categories, no time axis. Pure column-chart territory.

2. Line. Time on the X-axis → line, almost always.

3. Pie — just about. Three slices, parts of a whole. A stacked bar would also work and is often clearer. Avoid pie if you can't tell which slice is biggest at a glance.

4. Stacked bar. Comparison and composition combined — exactly what stacked bar is for. One bar per region, segments coloured by plan.

15 minutes — the final rehearsal

Say it out loud

Three questions about the pivots and charts you just built. Practise them out loud now — the answers are short, but the assessor wants to hear that every choice was deliberate.

Q1 — Why this PivotTable layout, not another?

The assessor will pick one of your pivots and ask. Your answer names the row/column/values choices and what question each one answers. "I put region in Rows and plan_type in Columns because Priya wants to see plans side by side per region. I put Count of record_id in Values because the question was 'how many sessions'. If she wanted total minutes I'd swap Count for Sum of duration."

Q2 — Why this chart, not another?

The Chart Test, narrated. "It's a comparison across regions, so column or bar — and there's no time element. Eleven regions is fine for a column chart, would have been too many for a pie. Pie charts only work for ≤5 slices anyway." Pick one of your charts and rehearse this answer.

Q3 — Why Power Query rather than just Excel?

The one-sentence answer is about Refresh. "Because next month, when new source data arrives, I overwrite the source file and click Data → Refresh All. The whole pipeline reruns — cleaning, merging, formulas, pivots, charts — in under thirty seconds. If I'd done this in Excel formulas alone, that would be half a day of rework, probably with a bug or two introduced."

Distinction tell

For every PivotTable, be ready to justify three things: what's in Rows and why, what's in Values and why, what aggregation (Sum, Count, Average) and why. Three sentences. The pivot looks identical to anyone else's — but the assessor can hear in your answer that you chose every part of it deliberately.
