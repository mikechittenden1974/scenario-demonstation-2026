two distinction descriptors live here

D2 and D3 in plain English

Two of the three Distinction descriptors sit in this content area. Both have the same shape underneath: evaluate the choice, justify it, name what would have gone wrong otherwise.

D2

"Critically evaluate why data is communicated and why different communication methods are used."

The two moves:

  1. Justify why you communicated this particular finding at all.
  2. Justify why you chose this delivery method for this audience.

D3

"Evaluate storage, management and distribution needs and justify ethical and security standards."

The two moves:

  1. Justify the storage / access decision (who sees what).
  2. Name the legal / ethical reason it was the right call.

The Audience Switch covers D2; the GDPR-aware version covers D3

When you walk through the Audience Switch in the discussion, add one D3 layer: "the version for the end user had names redacted — this is personal viewing data, UK GDPR; the version for the manager kept names because the business reason was set up under our access agreement." That single sentence ticks D3.

ASSESSOR FAVOURITE

Naming UK GDPR, data minimisation, or least-privilege access — even once — lifts D3 toward Distinction. These aren't jargon; they're the named principles the standard expects you to recognise.

the documentation skill

S11 — technical documentation

S11 is "Mandatory evidence: Present" — the assessor needs to hear about specific documents you've written using organisational templates. Not what documentation is for. Specific docs.

Three types that almost always count

  • Methodology notes — the paragraph at the bottom of a dashboard explaining sources, transforms, assumptions, refresh date. (You wrote one in Phase 1 Day 7.)
  • Data dictionaries — the table that names each field, its meaning, its allowed values, its source.
  • Standard operating procedures — the step-by-step doc for recurring tasks (e.g., monthly reconciliation).

What "organisational templates" means in practice

Most workplaces have a template you're expected to use — a header block with version, author, approval, status. The assessor wants to hear that you used the workplace template, not that you invented your own.

Question shape to expect: "Give me an example of a piece of technical documentation you produced."

The answer shape: name the document by type and title · name when it was used and by whom · mention the template / version-control approach · brief Reflection on what it taught you about writing for handover.

the layer that bolts d3 on

K13 — the legal & ethical layer

K13 is wide. The discussion-relevant chunks are smaller. Three areas the assessor will probe — with the answer-shape for each.

1
UK GDPR + Data Protection Act 2018

Personal data, data minimisation, lawful basis, retention. The answer shape: name one workplace example where you considered GDPR — redacting names in a shared sheet, aggregating instead of listing, deleting an unused export.

2
Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

Who owns the dashboards / queries / scripts you build at work? (Usually your employer, under your contract.) Who owns the data? (Depends on the source — you've probably used internal data, customer data, external data with different ownership.) The answer shape: one sentence acknowledging the ownership and one example of how it shaped a decision.

3
Data sharing + marketing consent

Sharing data with third parties (cloud providers, external analysts, partners) requires documented agreements. Marketing consent must be specific and opt-in. The answer shape: one workplace example — even if the example is "we considered sharing X with Y and decided not to because…".

RED FLAG · DON'T BLUFF GDPR

If you're not certain of a detail, say so. "My understanding is that lawful basis matters, but I'd want to check the specifics with our data protection lead." Honest beats wrong. Bluffing on GDPR specifics is a recurring failure mode.

There are no Dumb Questions

Q:I've only ever communicated my work to one audience — my manager. Can I still do the Audience Switch?
A:Yes — describe how you would communicate it differently to a peer or end user. The Audience Switch is about audience awareness, not about whether you've actually done all three. "To a technical peer I'd have included the methodology; to an end user I'd have skipped the percentage and just said 'fewer people'."

Q:My organisation doesn't use formal documentation templates. How do I answer S11?
A:You probably do have de-facto templates — e.g., the layout of dashboards, the format of methodology paragraphs, the structure of email summaries. Name those. "Our team has a standard methodology paragraph that lives at the bottom of every dashboard — sources, transforms, assumptions, last refresh."

Q:How much GDPR detail do I need to know?
A:Enough to name three things in your own work that show data-protection thinking: redacting names, aggregating data, limiting access. You're not being assessed as a DPO; you're being assessed as a data tech who knows the rules apply.
Q:I've sometimes used personal data without thinking about it — I just use what's in the source system. Is that a problem?
A:For the discussion, no — provided you can describe how you'd think about it now. Reflection moves like "on reflection, I'd want to check the lawful basis for that report" demonstrate exactly what D3 rewards: evaluation, not perfection.
Q:S12 is about "storage and security" — we just save things in our shared drive. What do I say?
A:That's storage and security — explain the controls on the shared drive. "Our shared drive is restricted to the team; the methodology folder requires manager approval to access." Storage / security doesn't mean cloud architecture — it means controlled access.

15 minutes — rehearse two STARRs

Say it for real

Q1 — Tell me about a time you had to communicate a finding to a different audience than the people you usually work with.

The shape: full STARR. The Audience Switch lives in Action: "For my manager I summarised at the headline level; for the customer-facing team I cut the percentages and replaced them with plain language." Reflection closes with D2: "the reason for splitting it that way was that…"

Q2 — Walk me through how you handle the security and storage of the data you work with.

The shape: two short stories. One about access (who can see the file). One about retention (when it gets deleted or aggregated). Land on a D3 sentence: "the reason for restricting access at that level was the lawful basis for the original collection — the data was gathered under a clinical agreement, not a marketing one."

SHOW THIS OFF

Naming "data minimisation", "least-privilege access", or "lawful basis" — once — tells the assessor you know the principles, not just the practices. One of those phrases is worth a paragraph of vague description.
