making invisible relationships visible

Scatter plots

A scatter plot is the simplest possible chart: every row of your data becomes one dot, positioned by two columns. The eye does the rest of the work — patterns that are impossible to see in a column of numbers jump out the moment they're plotted.

The setup

You need exactly two numeric columns from your data. The convention:

  • X-axis (horizontal): the column you think might be driving the change. Often something stable — tenure, age, plan type.
  • Y-axis (vertical): the column you think might be changing in response. Often the thing you care about — watch time, revenue, churn rate.

Drawing one in Excel

Select both columns (X first, then Y). Insert → Charts → Scatter → pick the first option (Scatter, dots only — no lines). Done. Excel plots one dot per row.

What to look for

Before doing any maths, look at the shape:

  • Dots climbing up-and-right. Positive relationship — as X goes up, Y goes up too.
  • Dots falling down-and-right. Negative relationship — as X goes up, Y goes down.
  • Dots in a blob. No relationship — they don't move together.
  • Dots in a curve. A relationship, but not a straight line — a trendline won't fit it well.
SHOW THIS OFF

Always describe the chart in plain English before you read the numbers off it. "The dots climb up and right, so longer-tenure subscribers do appear to watch more. Let's see if the maths agrees." The eye-test gives the assessor your interpretation, the maths confirms it.

the line that summarises the pattern

y = mx + c

Once your scatter plot shows a roughly straight-line pattern, the line of best fit is a single line drawn through the dots that minimises the total distance to all of them. It summarises the pattern in two numbers: a slope (m) and an intercept (c).

In plain English

  • Slope (m) = how much Y changes when X goes up by 1. If m = 1.4 hours per month, then every extra month of tenure adds about 1.4 watch hours.
  • Intercept (c) = where the line crosses the Y-axis. The "starting value" when X = 0. If c = 9.2 hours, a brand-new subscriber (zero tenure) is predicted to watch about 9.2 hours.

Together, the line is y = mx + c. For our example: hours = 1.4 × tenure + 9.2. Plug any tenure value in, get a predicted hours value out. That's all the maths is doing.

A line drawn through a scattering of dots summarises the relationship in two numbers. The dots don't all sit on the line, but the line is the best straight-line story we can tell about them.

three ticks — three numbers

Add a trendline in Excel

You don't draw the line of best fit yourself. Excel does it. Three clicks, and the equation and R² appear right on the chart.

The procedure

  1. Right-click any dot on your scatter plot → Add Trendline… A side pane opens.
  2. Trendline Options → Linear (the default). Other options exist (exponential, polynomial) — start with linear; reach for others only if the dots are obviously curved.
  3. At the bottom of the same pane: tick Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value on chart. Both appear in the chart area, usually near the line.

That's the entire output. The equation gives you slope (1.4) and intercept (9.2). The R² gives you the trust score for the line — covered on the next page.

how much of the story does the line tell?

R² — the trust score

The trendline is a summary of a relationship. R² tells you how good that summary is. It's a number between 0 and 1: how much of the variation in Y is explained by X.

The scale, in plain English

  • R² = 1.0 — every dot sits exactly on the line. Y is perfectly predicted by X. Almost never happens with real data.
  • R² ≈ 0.7+ — strong relationship. The line is a good summary; X explains most of what Y is doing.
  • R² ≈ 0.4 to 0.7 — moderate relationship. There's something there, but X is only part of the story. Other factors matter too.
  • R² ≈ 0.1 to 0.4 — weak relationship. The line tilts but the dots are scattered all over. Don't lean on the line.
  • R² ≈ 0 — no relationship. Don't quote the equation. The dots are a blob.
ASSESSOR FAVOURITE

If asked "what does an R² of 0.71 mean?", the one-sentence answer: "It means about 71% of the variation in Y is explained by X. The other 29% is down to other factors not in this analysis." Strong, plain English, no jargon.

when you need the numbers without the chart

SLOPE, INTERCEPT, RSQ

The trendline puts the three numbers on the chart. Sometimes you want them as values in a summary table — for example, to compare slopes across regions or plans. Three Excel formulas do exactly that.

Function Returns Syntax
SLOPE The slope (m) =SLOPE(known_y, known_x)
INTERCEPT The intercept (c) =INTERCEPT(known_y, known_x)
RSQ R² — the trust score =RSQ(known_y, known_x)

Be careful with argument order

All three functions take Y first, then X. That's the opposite of how you read the equation y = mx + c. It's the number-one mistake people make. To compute the relationship between tenure and watch hours, where hours is Y and tenure is X:

=SLOPE(tblSummary[watch_hours], tblSummary[tenure_months])

A useful bonus — CORREL

Closely related: =CORREL(range1, range2) returns the Pearson correlation coefficient — a number from -1 to +1 that measures the direction and strength of the linear relationship. CORREL squared equals RSQ. CORREL keeps the sign (negative for "as X goes up, Y goes down"), R² doesn't.

SHOW THIS OFF

When building a summary table, prefer the formulas to the chart numbers. "I used =SLOPE, =INTERCEPT and =RSQ so the three numbers live in cells I can reference. The chart is for the audience; the cells are for the methodology."

where a good line of best fit goes wrong

Three pitfalls

Regression looks like maths so people trust it more than they should. Three things can wreck your conclusion even when the R² looks good.

1. Correlation isn't causation

Two things moving together doesn't mean one causes the other. Subscribers who watch a lot of Drama might also rate Drama highly — but watching more doesn't cause them to rate higher. A third factor (they like Drama in the first place) drives both. Always ask: "could there be a lurking variable here?" If you can think of one, name it. Don't claim causation from a scatter plot.

2. Tiny samples lie

An R² of 0.95 from four data points is meaningless. A line will always fit a small number of dots well — that's just geometry, not insight. As a rough rule: at least 20–30 paired observations before a high R² means anything. If you have fewer, report the chart and the equation but be loud about the sample size.

3. Cherry-picked ranges

The relationship between tenure and hours might be strong over the first 12 months and then flatten out. If you only plot the first 12 months, you'll get a great R² and a confident slope — and you'll be wrong about everyone over a year tenure. Always plot the full range your data covers, even if it's messier.

ASSESSOR FAVOURITE

Whenever you present a regression finding, end the sentence with a caveat. "Tenure explains 71% of watch hours variation — but there's likely a lurking variable around plan type that I haven't accounted for." The caveat builds credibility. It shows you understand the limits of the analysis.

the brain-power answer, finally

Worked example

Back to the question from page 1: do longer-tenure subscribers watch more? Here's how you'd answer it on the StreamWave data — step by step.

Build the summary table

On a new sheet, build two columns:

  • tenure_months per subscriber. Use =DATEDIF(tblSubs[join_date], TODAY(), "M") or similar — months between join date and today.
  • total_watch_hours per subscriber. =SUMIF(tblActivity[subscriber_id], A2, tblActivity[duration]) / 60.

You now have one row per subscriber with both numbers. Couple of hundred rows — plenty for a meaningful regression.

Plot, fit, read

Select both columns → Insert → Scatter. Right-click a dot → Add Trendline → Linear → tick both display options. The chart shows something like:

y = 1.4x + 9.2     R² = 0.71

Interpret in plain English

"Each extra month of tenure is associated with about 1.4 more total watch hours. A brand-new subscriber is predicted to watch about 9.2 hours. R² of 0.71 means tenure explains about 71% of the variation in hours — there's clearly a relationship, though plan type and region almost certainly play a role too."

Three numbers, one sentence of plain English, one caveat. That's a finding ready for Priya's dashboard.

There are no Dumb Questions

Q:My scatter plot is curved, not straight. Can I still fit a linear trendline?
A:You can, but the R² will be low and the line will be a poor summary. Excel's Add Trendline pane offers other options (exponential, polynomial, power, logarithmic). If the data clearly curves, pick the matching shape. If you're not sure which to pick, the eye-test is reasonable — fit two or three options, see which R² is highest, but be cautious: a higher-order polynomial will always fit more closely. Don't over-fit.
Q:What if Y depends on more than one X? Can Excel handle that?
A:That's multiple regression — beyond Level 3 scope, and beyond what a trendline can show. The honest answer when you suspect multiple drivers: name the other likely variables in your caveat ("plan type and region likely matter too"), and either segment your scatter plot (one per plan type) or flag it as a question for further analysis.
Q:What's a "good" R²?
A:Depends on the field. Physics often sees 0.95+; behavioural data (how people watch TV) might top out at 0.4–0.6 and still be valuable. The number isn't good or bad on its own — what matters is whether it's useful for the decision.
Q:Why does SLOPE want Y first, then X?
A:Annoying, isn't it? Excel uses known_y's and known_x's — known outputs first, known inputs second. Just memorise that all three functions (SLOPE, INTERCEPT, RSQ) take Y first. If you swap them you'll get nonsense — typically a wildly different slope and a similarly wrong intercept.
Q:Should regression go on the dashboard, or in supporting notes?
A:The chart with the trendline can go on the dashboard if it tells a clear story. The bare equation usually doesn't — it belongs in supporting documentation. Show the picture, narrate the finding ("longer-tenure subscribers watch more"), and have the equation and R² available if anyone asks.

Three numbers — slope, intercept, R². One sentence in plain English. One caveat. That's a finding.

10 minutes — never skip

Say it out loud

Regression is one of those topics where the assessor wants to hear plain English, not algebra. Practise these three out loud.

Q1 — Walk me through what your trendline equation means.

Read it as a sentence, not as maths. "The equation is y = 1.4x + 9.2. The slope of 1.4 means each extra month of tenure is associated with about 1.4 more watch hours. The intercept of 9.2 means a brand-new subscriber is predicted to watch about 9.2 hours." Always translate slope and intercept into the units of your actual columns.

Q2 — How trustworthy is your line?

This is the R² question, in disguise. Two sentences. "R² is 0.71, which means tenure explains about 71% of the variation in watch hours. The remaining 29% is down to other factors not in this analysis — plan type and region being two likely candidates." Always name what the analysis doesn't account for.

Q3 — What could be a lurking variable?

The shape: name a plausible third factor and explain why it might matter. For tenure vs hours: "Plan type. Premium subscribers might both stay longer (higher tenure) AND watch more (more hours) because they're more engaged in the first place. Tenure and hours would correlate, but the underlying driver is engagement, not tenure itself." Naming lurking variables is what makes a competent analysis look like a senior one.

Distinction tell

For every regression you present, narrate the three numbers + one caveat. Slope (what each unit of X buys you). Intercept (the starting value). R² (how trustworthy the line is). Caveat (what's not in the analysis). Four sentences. That's distinction band.

Bullet Points — the chapter in 30 seconds

  • Scatter plot first. Two columns, one chart, eye-test before maths.
  • Line of best fit summarises the pattern in two numbers — slope (m) and intercept (c).
  • R² is the trust score. 0 = no pattern, 0.7+ = strong, 1.0 = perfect (and probably suspicious).
  • Three formulas: =SLOPE, =INTERCEPT, =RSQ. Y comes first. Always.
  • Correlation isn't causation. Every regression finding ends with a caveat about lurking variables.
  • Translate into plain English. Read the equation as a sentence, not as maths.
